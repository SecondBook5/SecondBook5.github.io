Research

Inferring biological state from incomplete measurements

Human biology is usually observed indirectly, asynchronously, and after the fact. I develop and apply models that make the measurement process explicit, recover interpretable latent state, and test how far the resulting inference can legitimately go.

Quantitative imaging · Host biology

HostBio / Comp-MRF

Using routine CT as a quantitative measurement of host tissue state

HostBio began with a measurement problem. Clinical CT is abundant and longitudinal, but voxel intensity is not biology: acquisition settings, contrast, tissue mixing, and anatomy all distort the signal. I wanted a representation of host tissue composition that could be interpreted biologically rather than treated as another black-box image feature.

Cross-modal transfer, not copy-and-paste

I conceived Comp-MRF after recognizing that tissue-mixture problems in CT share useful mathematical structure with deconvolution problems I had encountered in spatial transcriptomics. The analogy is incomplete, so the imaging model changes accordingly. Comp-MRF explicitly models partial-volume composition, spatial dependence, nuisance components, and within-scan reference tissues instead of assuming that an intensity threshold corresponds directly to a tissue class.

From method to research infrastructure

I engineered the framework into HostBio, a reusable quantitative-imaging platform implemented for MSK clinical research through MIM Development. The same logic has been extended from thymic composition to multi-organ host phenotyping across muscle, adipose tissue, liver, adrenal, thyroid, and additional compartments.

Biological validation

The model matters only if its outputs correspond to meaningful host biology. I therefore integrate imaging-derived phenotypes with longitudinal flow cytometry, cytokine profiling, tumor genomics, treatment exposure, and outcomes. Across lung-cancer cohorts, this work has connected thymic involution and broader host composition to peripheral immune state, treatment toxicity, disease control, and host immunometabolic phenotypes.

Broader imaging program

Related work extends the same measurement-first perspective to deep-learning segmentation, pretrained CT risk models, and longitudinal cone-beam CT. My role includes supporting nnU-Net v2 thymus-segmentation training and deployment, applying Sybil to lung-cancer risk analyses, and developing temporal imaging pipelines using radiomics, geometry, deformation, mixed-effects modeling, joint longitudinal-survival analysis, and dynamic prediction.

Spatial genomics · Regulatory dynamics

StageBridge

What can a static human tissue sample actually tell us about progression?

For my M.S. thesis in Bioinformatics at Johns Hopkins University, I developed StageBridge to study premalignant epithelial progression from cross-sectional spatial and single-nucleus transcriptomic data. The central difficulty is not generating a trajectory. It is deciding which aspects of a dynamic process are actually constrained by static observations.

Regulatory state in tissue context

StageBridge represents epithelial state using transcription-factor and pathway activities together with gene-regulatory structure, then conditions that state on local immune, stromal, extracellular-matrix, inflammatory, and spatial context. The goal is to ask whether different tissue contexts are associated with different regulatory transitions, not merely whether cells can be ordered along a pseudotime axis.

A failure that changed the method

An early context-neutral transport assumption looked plausible in an abstract latent benchmark but failed after projection into biological regulatory space. Prespecified recoverability testing fell to a Tier-2 cosine of 0.27. I redesigned the coupling using conditional stratification, improving Tier-2 recovery to 0.84. More importantly, the failure changed the scientific claim: the framework was restricted to a supported conditional-transport estimand rather than interpreted as an individual causal trajectory.

Inference boundary

Cross-sectional human tissue does not uniquely determine longitudinal dynamics. StageBridge therefore treats uncertainty and non-identifiability as part of the scientific problem. I evaluate robustness with patient-held-out analyses, matched-context shuffles, degeneracy controls, and cross-disease testing to distinguish reproducible context-associated signal from unsupported causal reconstruction.

Multi-omics · Chronic inflammation

Secondary lymphedema

Persistent tissue remodeling after inflammatory injury

In collaboration with the Mehrara laboratory at Memorial Sloan Kettering, I study how epithelial, stromal, immune, and lymphatic compartments remain altered after chronic lymphatic injury in secondary lymphedema. The system provides a complementary way to study persistence after injury across molecular and cellular scales.

Computational scope

  • Paired-donor single-cell transcriptomics of normal and lymphedematous human skin.
  • Bulk RNA-seq of lymphatic injury and repair.
  • Label-free proteomics of healthy and diseased tissue.
  • Cross-cohort microbiome analysis with measurement-aware harmonization.

Measurement artifacts can look biological

One recurring lesson from this work is that cross-cohort differences can be created by the analysis pipeline itself. In microbiome data, an apparent approximately two-fold difference in observed genus diversity largely disappeared after harmonized taxonomic classification. That failure mode motivated Dysbioscope, a reproducible workflow designed to make measurement and processing choices explicit before biological interpretation.

Why this belongs in my broader research program

The scientific question is not simply which genes or proteins change in lymphedema. It is which regulatory programs stabilize persistent tissue states, which compartments carry those states, and which apparent signatures survive donor-aware, cross-modal, and measurement-aware analysis.

Public work

Published studies have examined PAR2-associated epidermal remodeling and the broader omic architecture of secondary lymphedema. Ongoing analyses are described here only at a level appropriate for public disclosure.
